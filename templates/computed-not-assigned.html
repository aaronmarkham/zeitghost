On figures, fields, and one idea borrowed from geometry

Computed,
not assigned.

Nobody thinks about the whole sky. You pick out a figure — a handful of stars and the lines between them — give it a name, and from then on you can point at it, hand it to someone else, and reason inside it without loading the rest. This is about what happens when you do that to information, and where the idea for doing it came from.

01 · THE FIELD

Everything visible is not the same as anything thinkable

This is why graph databases seduce and then stall. The inspector renders the whole field at once, every node equally bright, and it is genuinely beautiful for about a week. Then comes the question nobody has a good answer to: now what? There is nothing to hold. No figure, nothing named, nothing bounded, nothing you can come back to next month and know means the same thing.

Pick a figure below. The rest of the sky doesn't go away — it just stops competing for attention.

The stars drift the whole time you watch — every one of them, slowly, in its own direction. The figure holds anyway. What's stored is which stars and which lines, never where they happened to be.

That last part is the one worth sitting with. Orion has been Orion for as long as anyone has written it down, and not one of its stars has stayed put. The figure is stable because it was never a description of positions. It's a selection and a set of relationships — and those are cheap to keep, cheap to name, and survive everything underneath them moving.

02 · WHERE THE IDEA CAME FROM

Knowing when the list is finished

Connor Hill, a high school student in State College, Pennsylvania, won the 2026 Regeneron Science Talent Search for closing a problem that had stayed open since the 1870s: every noble polyhedron, meaning every solid so uniform that all its faces are alike and all its corners are alike. By 2020 people had found 61 of them, one at a time, with no way to know whether the next one was out there. He proved there are exactly 146, plus two endless families.

The interesting part isn't the count — it's how you get to be sure of a count. His method parametrizes the orbits of symmetry groups acting on space, then defines an equivalence relation on those orbits. Enumerate the classes and you have enumerated the solids, because two things in the same class were never two things.

That is the move this page is about, and it arrives with a guarantee attached: no double-counting, and nothing missed.

Which is easier to watch than to describe. The solid on the right is written down as a single face — the violet one, its seed — plus three rotations that leave the whole shape looking unchanged. Call them a, b and c. Apply all three to the seed and new faces appear; apply them again to those and more appear, and the readout names the sequence of turns that reached each one. Read a → b → c as an instruction: from the seed, turn by a, then by b, then by c, and you land on that face.

It stops on its own at twelve, because by then every further rotation lands on a face already found. One face and three turns is the entire record — the other eleven are worked out on demand, and knowing to stop at twelve is not a judgement call. That is what the method buys.

Worked out so far: 1 face

03 · THE CROSSING

A figure with no polyhedron in it

The solids were never the point — they were the most visible case of the point, which is that a relationship can hold steady while everything expressing it moves. Take that away from geometry entirely and it still works.

Take a closed loop of see-also references: five headings, each pointing to the next, the last back to the first. A constellation, essentially. Nothing in it says where to begin or which way to read, so two people can record the same loop and produce different strings. Rotate it or flip it: the as recorded line changes every time, while the authorized form and its key underneath never do.

Which is authority control, arrived at sideways. Librarianship has collocated variant forms under one authorized heading since Cutter set out the objective in 1876 — and it canonicalizes composed structures too, through citation order in subject strings and Ranganathan's facet formula. Both work the same way: type each part, then prescribe an order by type.

What the geometry adds is the case where the parts carry no distinguishing role at all. A loop has no first element and no direction; a grouping of co-equal terms has no principal member. There is no facet to assign, so an order cannot be prescribed — it has to be computed. And a computed representative has a property a prescribed one lacks: two systems that have never communicated arrive at the same answer, with no cataloguer in between.

04 · MEASURED

The case scoring cannot reach

The obvious objection is that none of this is necessary — that a similarity score with a sensible cutoff would settle the same records without the apparatus. So it was measured, against the alternative, on the structures the apparatus is actually for.

Two hundred pairs across three collections: undirected rings, directed cycles where reversal means something different, and unordered groupings. Every pair carries known ground truth. A rotated ring is the same ring. A ring with two headings swapped is not. Each pair is then decided twice — once by comparing canonical keys, once by scoring the text as written.

Which explains the shape of the failure. Similarity here is not a weak signal waiting to be tuned — it points the wrong way. Rewriting a structure changes the text a great deal; altering the structure changes it hardly at all. No cutoff repairs a signal that is anti-correlated with the truth.

The rule's 114 of 114 is true by construction and carries no information on its own — canonicalization is exact, so anything less would be a bug rather than a tuning problem. The column that means something is the wrong merges, because a rule that collapsed distinct structures would post perfect recall too, and be useless. Both numbers only mean anything together. And this measures structural variation only: case, whitespace, typos and unicode drift are a different problem, one that scoring handles well. Neither number belongs in the other's sentence.

05 · NOT A NEW IDEA

The same move, made five times over

Nothing here is an invention. Computing one agreed form for a structure is a technique that separate fields have arrived at independently, mostly without reading each other, over about fifty years.

Chemistry — canonical SMILES, and later InChI.

One molecular structure, one string, so any two databases naming the same compound produce the same identifier. In industrial use for decades.

Graph theory — canonical labelling, as in nauty and Traces.

Relabel a graph into the one form all its equivalent labellings share. Refined continuously since the 1970s.

The semantic web — RDF dataset canonicalization.

Put a graph into a fixed form so it can be hashed and signed, and so two parties can verify they hold the same thing.

String algorithms — Booth's least-rotation algorithm, 1980.

The exact routine settling the rings on this page. Textbook material, older than most of the systems that would benefit from it.

Cataloguing — citation order, and Ranganathan's facet formula.

Compound subjects reduced to one prescribed string. The oldest entry on the list by a wide margin, and the only one built for people rather than machines.

Convergence like that is the strongest evidence a technique gets. Five fields reaching the same move independently means the problem is real and the solution close to forced — not a fashion.

And every one of those adoptions happened under the same condition: two parties needing to agree on identity without coordinating first. That is the trigger, and it also marks where the technique isn't needed.

06 · WHAT IT'S FOR

Twenty rules outlast twenty million names

You can always register an identifier instead of computing one. Wikidata does exactly that — every item gets an assigned code, and the world maps into it. It works extremely well, and it knows things no computed form ever will, such as that Twain and Clemens are one person.

But a registry presumes three things: that it exists, that everyone can reach it, and that someone maintains it. The case this was built for has none of them — independent programs holding fragments of data and a handful of shared rules, with no backend to consult and no authority to ask. A computed form is the only kind of agreement two parties can reach without having met.

Which is the property worth naming, because everything on this page quietly depends on it. The solid is a single face and three turns. The ring is five headings and a statement of which differences don't count. In both cases the artifact carries its own construction rules, so whatever picks it up can work with it from itself alone — no schema to fetch, no documentation to consult, no prior arrangement with whoever wrote it.

The argument is a familiar one in a different register. Description held in the object or description held in the catalogue; metadata embedded in the file or resolved from a record elsewhere; a preservation package built to be intelligible after the system that made it is gone. The question of how much a thing should carry about itself is not new, and the answer has never been all or nothing.

What a self-describing form does is not remove the need for agreement — it moves it. Everyone still has to agree on the handful of primitives: what the operations are, what counts as a difference, how the parts combine. But that is a small set, settled once, and it does not grow. The alternative is agreeing on the identifiers themselves, which is an enormous set that grows forever and needs somebody to keep it. Twenty rules held in common will outlast twenty million names held in a register.

Two things it will not do

It runs one way.

Every turn of a solid can be undone; almost no real normalization can. Dropping dates or folding case throws away something it cannot put back, so this moves from variant toward authorized and never back out.

It reads no meaning.

It collapses only the differences you can state in advance. Everything else still needs scoring, or judgment, or a person — and no set of primitives will ever let something derive from first principles that Twain and Clemens were one man.

So let the shape of a thing carry its own rules, and leave what it means to the people who know. An idea travelled out of a mathematics paper about solids, through a filing rule, into working code that settles every structural variant put to it and wrongly merges none — and picked up a way of seeing along the way that turns out to describe half the hard problems in keeping information over time. Figures over fields. The rule rather than the copies. What holds while everything underneath it drifts.
